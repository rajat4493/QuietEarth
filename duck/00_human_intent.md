# TheDuck — Human Intent Translation

## Human intent
Meditation is confusing because it is often presented as a universal method or mystical trance. The product should begin with the opposite assumption: different minds and different states of mind may need different entry practices.

The app should help a user understand how their attention behaves using both self-report and optional behavioral evidence, challenge the user's own self-description when the evidence conflicts, then prescribe a short meditation experiment and verify whether it actually helps.

## Product promise
“Understand how your mind moves. Practice accordingly.”

## User outcome
After onboarding, the user should be able to say:
- I understand what the app currently thinks my attention pattern is.
- I can see what evidence supports that view.
- I can see alternative interpretations.
- I know why today’s meditation was chosen for me.
- I can complete the practice in under 10 minutes.
- I can report what happened in under 30 seconds.
- The app can revise its hypothesis rather than pretending it was always correct.

## Non-negotiable philosophy
Do not measure the user's mind with language models. Ask them only to describe observable behaviour, treat those descriptions as hypotheses, and let practice supply the evidence.
Do not manufacture precision. A number the product did not measure is never shown.
Do not reduce the user to a permanent personality type.
Treat attention patterns as hypotheses that can change by context and over time.
Do not confuse dharana, dhyana, samadhi, mindfulness, therapy, or diagnosis.
Use Sanskrit/Yogic concepts carefully as interpretive parallels, not medical labels.
