# Practice Content Model — Classical Source, Modern Evidence, One Simple Screen

Answers the open question: how to give each attention pattern a real meditation technique, grounded in
Patañjali and Vedic practice *and* in current science, without becoming either a scripture app or a
pseudo-clinical one.

The short answer: **the wrapper already exists, and Patañjali wrote it.**

---

## 1. The structural insight

`Yoga Sūtra` 1.30–1.39 is not a list of teachings that happen to be about meditation. It is, in
structure, exactly this product:

| Sūtra | What it does | QuietEarth equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| 1.30 | Names nine obstacles (`antarāyāḥ`) that scatter the mind | The attention patterns — states, not identities |
| 1.31 | Names their accompaniments: distress, negative mood, bodily unsteadiness, disturbed breathing | The secondary dimensions (arousal, emotional capture) |
| 1.32 | `tat-pratiṣedhārtham eka-tattva-abhyāsaḥ` — practise one principle against them | One practice at a time, not a library |
| 1.33–1.38 | A **menu of alternative methods**, each introduced with `vā` — "or" | The recommendation rules |
| 1.39 | `yathābhimata-dhyānād vā` — "or by meditating on whatever suits you" | The whole personalisation thesis |

That is a ~1,800-year-old text explicitly saying: *different minds need different entry practices, and
the right one is the one that suits this practitioner.* Patañjali does not prescribe one method. He
lists several and then says: or whatever works for you.

**So QuietEarth is not bolting modern personalisation onto ancient content. It is implementing the
structure the source text already has, with measurement the source text could not have.** That is the
wrapper, it is honest, and it is a far better story than "ancient wisdom meets AI".

The obstacle framing also solves a product problem for free: `antarāyāḥ` are **conditions that arise
and pass**, not personality types. That is precisely the stance `duck/00_human_intent.md` demands.

## 2. The scientific spine

Contemporary contemplative science organises practices by *what attention is doing*, which maps onto
the classical menu without forcing:

- **Focused attention (FA)** — sustained attention on a chosen object, with detect → disengage →
  redirect as the trained loop (Lutz, Slagter, Dunne & Davidson, *TiCS* 2008).
- **Open monitoring (OM)** — non-reactive moment-to-moment monitoring without a single object.
- **Compassion / loving-kindness** — now generally treated as a third family.
- **Automatic self-transcending** — effortless mantra practice, distinguished by alpha1 activity
  (Travis & Shear, *Consciousness and Cognition* 2010). This is where Vedic `japa` sits, and it is
  why a repeated phrase is a genuinely *different* mechanism from watching the breath, not a
  cosmetic variation.

MVP scope stays inside FA, the compassion family, and mantra-as-anchor. OM belongs to a later
milestone: it is harder to instruct safely in text and harder to evaluate in a seven-day window.

## 3. The mapping

Each practice cites a classical source and a modern family. Nothing is invented to fill a cell.

| Pattern | Classical obstacle | Sūtra remedy | Modern family | Practice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High switching / branching | `vikṣepa` — scattering | 1.35 `viṣayavatī` — a sense-based object that binds the mind | FA, detect-and-return | **Return Training** |
| Switching + restlessness | `vikṣepa` with 1.31 `aṅgamejayatva` (unsteady body), `śvāsa-praśvāsa` (disturbed breath) | 1.34 `pracchardana-vidhāraṇābhyāṃ vā prāṇasya` — gentle exhale and pause | Autonomic settling, then FA | **Settle Then Focus** |
| Dullness / low energy | `styāna` (mental heaviness), `ālasya` (inertia); commentarial `laya` (sinking) | 1.36 `viśokā vā jyotiṣmatī` — the luminous, sorrowless; classically taken as brightening, upright posture, raised gaze | Arousal up, then FA | **Energize Then Attend** |
| Emotional capture | 1.31 `daurmanasya` — despondency | 1.33 `maitrī-karuṇā-muditā-upekṣā` — the four attitudes | Compassion / loving-kindness | **Emotional Clearing** |
| Sustained / one-pointed | Already `ekāgra` in the commentarial scale | 1.39 `yathābhimata`; toward 3.1–3.2 `dhāraṇā → dhyāna` | FA, extended | **Sustained Flow** |
| Insufficient evidence | — | 1.39 — "or whatever suits you" | Neutral | **Baseline Settling** |

**Anchors carry the Vedic thread.** A felt anchor (breath, contact, sound) is 1.35 `viṣayavatī`; a
repeated phrase or count is `japa` and belongs to Travis & Shear's fourth family. The existing
sensory-orientation rule already chooses between them — it now has a lineage as well as a reason.

The `1.34` remedy is exhale-lengthening only. That stays inside the breathing-safety rule in
`product/CONTENT_AND_SAFETY.md`: no forceful prāṇāyāma, no retention, no hyperventilation.

## 4. What the evidence actually supports — and what it does not

This section exists so no one writes a claim the research will not carry.

**Supported enough to build on:**
- Practices are mechanistically distinct, not interchangeable — the FA/OM/compassion and
  automatic-self-transcending taxonomies exist because the differences are measurable.
- People differ in which anchor suits them. In a study of 82 participants, 49% preferred the breath,
  30% an auditory phrase, and 21% a visual image (Frontiers in Psychology, 2018).
- **Most people cannot predict their own fit.** In that same study, over half ended up partial to a
  *different* anchor than the one their meditation-naïve bias picked. This is the single most
  important finding for QuietEarth: it is direct evidence that self-report alone cannot select a
  practice, and that trying is the only way to find out — which is exactly the loop already built.
- Brief practices produce modest improvements, and response is partly predictable from baseline
  characteristics (randomised trial, N = 5,049, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2026).

**Not established, and must never be claimed:**
- That assigning a practice by profile beats simply giving everyone a default. The 2026 trial that
  showed response is *predictable* explicitly calls for future trials to test algorithm-guided
  assignment. **Nobody has run that study.** QuietEarth is doing exactly the thing the literature
  says has not yet been tested.
- That meditation reliably improves attention. Meta-analytic results are small to moderate and
  inconsistent — some find effects on executive control, others find none on working memory.
- Anything about ADHD, anxiety, depression, or any clinical condition.

**The honest pitch, which is better than the dishonest one:**
> We don't know which practice suits you. Neither do you — the research says people guess their own
> fit wrong more than half the time. So we make our best evidence-based guess, tell you how we'd know
> if we were wrong, and let one week of your own practice decide.

That is a real, defensible position, and it is the same epistemic stance the rest of the product
already takes.

## 5. Safety

Between roughly 50% and 53% of regular meditators report at least one unpleasant or adverse effect,
and 6–14% report enduring negative effects (international cross-sectional study, *BJPsych Open*,
2021; Britton & Lindahl's *Varieties of Contemplative Experience* programme). This is not a fringe
finding and a prescribing app must not ignore it.

What follows for QuietEarth:
- The reflection already asks whether you feel *more scattered* afterwards. That question is a
  safety instrument, not only an evidence one — a repeated "more scattered" is a signal to change the
  practice, not to push through.
- Add an always-available **"this isn't working for me"** action that changes the practice immediately,
  without waiting for day seven. The seven-day rule governs *hypothesis* changes, not user comfort.
- Keep sessions short. The shortest templates are 4–5 minutes and that is deliberate.
- Static boundary copy stays: this is a meditation-learning product, not therapy or crisis support.

## 6. Presentation — simple by default, deep on demand

Three layers. The first is all a user ever has to meet.

**Layer 1 — Practice (default).** The instruction and the timer. Two or three sentences. No Sanskrit,
no citations, no science. Someone who never taps anything else still gets a complete, usable practice.

**Layer 2 — "Why this practice" (one tap).** Already built: the evidence statements behind the
routing decision, in plain words.

**Layer 3 — "Where this comes from" (one more tap).** A provenance card, and the only place lineage
and research appear:

> **Where this comes from**
> *Yoga Sūtra* 1.34 — `pracchardana-vidhāraṇābhyāṃ vā prāṇasya`
> "Or by the gentle exhalation and retention of breath."
> — translated by ⟨named edition⟩
>
> Patañjali lists this as *one of several* alternatives, not the method. Sūtra 1.39 ends the list with
> "or by meditating on whatever suits you."
>
> **Modern parallel** — settling the body before asking attention to steady is consistent with how
> focused-attention practice is described in contemporary research.
>
> **What this does not mean** — this is an interpretive parallel, not a medical claim, and not
> evidence that this practice works for you. That is what the week is for.

The caveat is part of the card, not a footnote. Per `product/CONTENT_AND_SAFETY.md`, no Sanskrit term
is ever a label for the user: the copy says "this resembles what the tradition calls `styāna`", never
"you are `styāna`".

**The one visual element — the lineage strip.** A single thin horizontal line per practice: the
classical source at one end, the modern parallel at the other, the practice itself in the middle. One
line, three labels, no ornament. It states the product's whole thesis — old structure, new
measurement — in a glance, reuses the existing attention-path motif language, renders fine in both
themes, and costs almost nothing to draw. This is the "best of both worlds" made visible without
adding visual weight.

## 7. Status

**Done.** The glosses are written and live in `ProjectStill/Practice/ClassicalReference.swift`. Every
one is our own plain-English rendering, labelled as such on the card, with a public-domain edition
(Johnston 1912, Jha 1907) named so a reader can check it against a scholarly source. No modern
translator's wording is reproduced anywhere, so the copyright question is closed.

The six practices are expanded from 185 words of instruction to roughly 720, delivered as **timed
cues** rather than a paragraph held on screen for four minutes. Each cue is placed at a fraction of
its step, so pacing scales with any session length: guidance arrives, then silence, then guidance.
That is the difference between a guided practice and a timer with a caption.

`ProvenanceCard` implements Layer 3, including the lineage strip.

## 8. What is still outstanding
- A Sanskritist's read of the glosses. They are careful and deliberately plain, but they are not
  scholarship and have not been checked by anyone qualified.
- Devanagari alongside transliteration, and diacritics rendered correctly at all Dynamic Type sizes.
- Whether the commentarial scale (`kṣipta, mūḍha, vikṣipta, ekāgra, niruddha`) appears at all. It maps
  seductively well onto the patterns, which is exactly why it is risky — it is commentarial rather
  than Patañjali's own, and it reads as a five-type personality system. Current recommendation: cite
  it in Layer 3 as a historical parallel, never as a label, or leave it out.
- Someone who actually teaches meditation reading the cue text aloud at pace, to check the silences
  land where they should.
- The "this isn't working for me" escape from §5 is specified but not built.
